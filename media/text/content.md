##### Section Context
## The Death and Life of Great American Cities
2026 marks the 65th anniversary of the 1961 publication of Jane Jacobs's *The Death and Life of Great American Cities*. Her first book, it remains Jacobs's most widely read and most influential. Its thesis was that cities were problems of "organized complexity" which thrived on the unplanned variety of street life. It challenged existing city planning orthodoxy, which held that cities must be carefully planned to keep homes far from dirty, unsafe business and industry.
<!-- svg: -->
The city planners of the early 20th century criticized urban spaces as dirty and crowded - and rightly so. The slums of New York, Boston and London, centered around polluting factories, were unsanitary and unpleasant. The air and water were polluted, and tuberculosis, typhoid and cholera spread quickly. The streets were often filled with manure from horse-drawn carriages. People slept six or more to a room in crowded tenements. Reformers like Jacob Riis documented these conditions, and cities responded with tenement laws, sanitation systems, and eventually zoning codes to separate land uses.
<!-- svg: -->
From these conditions, a new movement towards city planning began to take shape. Ebenezer Howard's "Garden City", Daniel Burnham's "City Beautiful" and Le Corbusier's "Radiant City" all imagined that cities could be planned to reduce overcrowding and pollution. Le Corbusier, in particular, envisioned widely-spaced high-rise towers in parks where residents would live, separate from the business towers and factories where they worked, with people traveling between them by car or mass transit. 
<!-- svg: -->
Following World War II, housing shortages led to a building boom that took up many of these principles. In Manhattan, huge complexes like Stuyvesant Town and Peter Cooper Village, and public housing like the Lillian Wald Houses and Jacob Riis houses followed Le Corbusier's tower-in-a-park model, though unlike in Le Corbusier's vision, they stood within the city. Working-class neighborhoods were demolished to build these, and existing residents were displaced. The Housing Act of 1949 funded "slum clearance", later called urban renewal, which in New York was run by Robert Moses. Manhattan's San Juan Hill, on the Upper West Side, another working-class neighborhood made up primarily of African-American, Afro-Caribbean and Puerto Rican residents, was cleared to make way for Lincoln Center.
<!-- svg: -->
Jacobs, who was raised in Scranton, Pennsylvania, moved to Greenwich Village in 1935 at 19. Through the 1950s she was a writer for Architectural Forum, where she saw this work up close and began to question the mainstream consensus on urban planning. Her neighborhood, parts of which had been labeled a slum and marked for clearance, was, to her, no slum at all. Instead, it was a working-class neighborhood where the streets were always full of people, where neighbors kept each other safe and where homes, shops and workplaces sat side by side, keeping the sidewalks busy from morning to night. In her book she described that daily rhythm in close detail, and it became her most enduring image: the sidewalk ballet.

## Short Blocks
>"Long blocks, in their nature, thwart the potential advantages that cities offer to incubation, experimentation, and many small or special enterprises, insofar as these depend upon drawing their customers or clients from among much larger cross-sections of passing public."(238)
##### Short Blocks
<!-- svg: map-a-zoom-out -->
We will start with the factor that has changed the least in 65 years: short blocks. 
<!-- svg: -->
Jacobs writes that short blocks give pedestrians frequent opportunities to turn corners, which means more possible routes any person can take to a given destination. A resident who goes to the same place regularly (whether it's their subway stop or favorite grocery store) will often take different routes to get there on different days, if the short blocks allow it, giving them more opportunities to see different parts of the neighborhood. Long blocks do the opposite, funneling people onto a few streets and leaving the rest quiet.
<!-- svg: map-a-zoom-in -->
Built before the standard 1811 Manhattan grid of widely spaced avenues (approx. 600-900 feet apart) and closely-spaced streets (approx 200-260 feet apart), blocks in Greenwich Village range between 150 - 500 feet and average 290 feet, among the shortest of all neighborhoods in Manhattan.
<!-- svg: chart-b -->
In addition to providing more opportunities for pedestrians to turn corners, the short, irregular blocks of Greenwich Village provide 
>"visual interruptions, cutting off the indefinite distant view and at the same time visually heightening and celebrating intense street use by giving it a hint of enclosure and entity".
<!-- svg: -->
Short blocks can also help a pedestrian's feeling of safety. A person walking on a short block who gets a feeling of being unsafe, either because of a person or their physical surroundings, can find somewhere to turn within a couple hundred feet, whereas a person walking on a long avenue might have no choice but to turn in the opposite direction.

##### A Mixture of Old and New Buildings 
<!-- svg: map-c -->
Following Dutch and English colonization of Lenape land, Greenwich Village grew from farmland into a built-up neighborhood in the early 1800s, which left it some of Manhattan's oldest surviving buildings. Much of that early construction is still standing. Although parts of the neighborhood were threatened with 'urban renewal' in the 1950s and 1960s, which would have demolished many older buildings, residents defeated those plans. In 1969 the city designated the Greenwich Village Historic District, protecting many of the neighborhood's older buildings from demolition.
<!-- svg: chart-d -->
Roughly 40 percent of the buildings in Greenwich Village were built before 1861. Another 50 percent were built between 1861 and 1929. Only about 10 percent of Greenwich Village's buildings were built after 1929. This lends the neighborhood a wide range of architectural styles, which makes it a pleasant and interesting place in which to walk around.
When Jacobs writes about a mixture of old and new buildings, though, it isn’t only for the purpose of visual diversity. Primarily, she writes that the buildings should 
>“vary in age and condition, including a good proportion of old ones so that they vary in the economic yield they must produce.” (197)

Just as there was a large variation in age of buildings, there was also a large variation in asking rents, with per square foot prices ranging from $2.33 per square foot per year to $9.68, according to New York Times Classified ads. Add in the fact that would-be renters could choose a larger or smaller storefront, depending on their needs, and you find that a prospective renter could pay as little as $68 per month in 1961 for a 350 square foot space, or as much as $350 per month for a larger, possibly nicer space. 

<!-- svg: chart-e e3 -->
Jacobs expected old buildings to supply the lower end of the rent range. In the first half of 2024, according to REBNY, asking rents for storefronts on Bleecker Street between 7th Avenue South and Houston ranged from $160 to $195 per square foot, with the most expensive space costing only 22% more than the least expensive one. This is despite almost all the buildings on that stretch being built before 1930.

<!-- svg: chart-e e2 -->
Although Greenwich Village is still a place with both old and new buildings, the buildings now do not vary in the economic yield they must produce. There is little difference between the cheapest and most expensive storefronts, leaving small and less profitable businesses priced out entirely.

Why would one of New York's most desireable neighborhoods be shrinking?

Not for lack of demand. People weren't moving out because they no longer wanted to live there. Instead, household size was shrinking, from 1.67 on average in 1970 to 1.5 today.  Households with at least 3 members went from 13.4% of the population in 1970 to just 11.4% in 2020.

That problem is compounded by the rising rents we've already seen. A business can't sustain itself when it has both rising expenses and a decreasing number of customers. Add in online shopping, and it becomes very difficult for niche businesses to stay open.

Jacobs's last condition for a vital neighborhood is mixed primary uses. She thought for a neighborhood to work well, it needed to have more than one primary function, and preferably more than two. ##### Why It's Happening
## Why It's Happening

To understand why so many storefronts sit empty in high-rent districts like Greenwich Village, it helps to know a little about the concept that economists call option value. In economics, option value refers to the economic value a property owner generates by retaining the flexibility to make a decision later rather than committing immediately to a long-term asset lock.

Imagine a landlord who suddenly has a vacant storefront for rent. It is the year 2008. They have potential tenants, who are willing to pay $130/square foot per year, comparable with rents in the past. However, this landlord knows that Marc Jacobs recently rented several storefronts just down the street for $600/square foot. By locking in a 10-year lease at $130/square foot, the landlord loses out on the possibility of getting $600/square foot for his storefront, a loss of many thousands of dollars over the next 10 years. You can easily imagine the landlord deciding not to rent the storefront out at $130, or $140, or $150 per square foot, instead choosing to wait for a tenant willing to pay more. 

When you imagine 10, 20 or 100 landlords all making the same calculation, suddenly it's easy to see how storefronts could sit empty for years on end.

In examining storefront vacancy in New York City, economists Erica Moszkowski and Daniel Stackman cite two sources of uncertainty which generate option value for landlords. The first, time series uncertainty, states that in a market where rents are often cyclical, landlords have an incentive to sign tenants as close to the top of the rent cycle as possible. The second, cross-sectional heterogeneity in tenant willingness to pay, also referred to as "match quality", states that there is substantial variation between what any two given tenants are willing to pay for a given space, creating an incentive for the landlord to wait for a higher-paying tenant rather than lease to a lower-paying one. (Moszkowski & Stackman, 2026)

###### Research underpinnings
## Do the four conditions matter?
Jacobs was not a researcher in the conventional sense. Though she completed classwork at Columbia, she left without earning a degree. Her expertise came from observing the city around her and drawing conclusions from what she saw. It's fair to ask whether there is any empirical evidence that her four conditions actually shape the economic success and social cohesion of a district, or if they are merely a romanticized snapshot of 1950s Greenwich Village.

In recent years, researchers have begun putting her theories to the test. In 2019, researchers in Barcelona used smartphone tracking to analyze urban vitality by tracking the daily walking itineraries of 241 young adults. They created a "JANE index", a synthetic indicator based on Jacobs' conditions, and found that neighborhoods scoring high on the index(possessing density, mixed primary uses, a mix of old and new buildings, high transit access, and a lack of border vacuums) strongly correlate with increased pedestrian activity (Delclòs-Alió et al., 2019). 

So neighborhoods with Jacobs's conditions have more pedestrian density. But does that increased foot traffic lead to the type of trust in your neighbors and community that Jacobs described in "The Death and Life of Great American Cities"? And does that type of trust make people happier?

In "Happy City", Charles Montgomery examined those two questions. Looking at research from around the world, he found that "People who live in monofunctional, car-dependent neighborhoods outside of urban centers are much less trusting of other people than people who live in walkable neighborhoods where housing is mixed with shops, services, and places to work"(need proper cite).

##### Conclusion
## Conclusion

When I embarked on this project I was ready to say that the gentrification and high-rent blight of Greenwich Village was something that Jacobs could never have predicted.Yet, in "The Death and Life of Great American Cities", she maps out the exact feedback loop currently playing out in the neighborhood. In a chapter called "The Self-Destruction of Diversity", Jacobs writes,
"Whichever form the self-destruction takes, this, in broad strokes, is what happens: A diversified mixture of uses at some place in the city becomes outstandingly popular and successful as a whole. Because of the location's success, which is invariably based on flourishing and magnetic diversity, ardent competition for space in this locality develops. It is taken up in what amounts to the economic equivalent of a fad. The winners in the competition for space will represent only a narrow segment of the many uses that together created success...Thus, from this process, one or few dominating uses finally emerge triumphant. But the triumph is hollow. A most intricate and successful organism of economic mutual support and social mutual support has been destroyed by this process... In time, a place that was once so successful and once the object of such ardent competition, wanes and becomes marginal."

Decades later, the experiences of local business owners echo her warning. When Richard Geist closed Uncle Sam's Army/Navy Store on West 8th street after decades in business, he blamed reduced foot traffic rather than high rents. As chronicled by Jeremiah Moss on Vanishing New York: "Geist told me the closure is by choice, mostly, and that 8th Street just isn't 8th Street anymore... High rents wiped out the shoe businesses here, leaving a blighted ghost town about 10 years ago... Geist says there's no foot traffic. The street is dead." (https://vanishingnewyork.blogspot.com/2018/12/uncle-sams-armynavy.html). The year before, Geist had told the New York Times, "Gentrification is killing us. Eighth Street has lost the magic" (as cited in Moss, [2018]).

The lost magic is not unique to 8th Street or to Greenwich Village. Across walkable, mixed-use neighborhoods in New York and beyond, independent storefronts are being displaced by capital-backed chains: fast-casual salad and burrito places, corporate coffee shops, and cookie-cutter retail. When financial speculation and monoculture strip away the fine-grained variety of the street, they destroy the mechanism that made the neighborhood resilient in the first place. And we can all make a difference in choosing how we show up in our neighborhoods - where we shop, how we interact with our community, and how we come together when our neighbors need us.

As Jacobs observed,
>"Cities have the capability of providing something for everybody, only because, and only when, they are created by everybody."
