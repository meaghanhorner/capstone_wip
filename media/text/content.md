##### Section Context
## Context

2026 marks the 65th anniversary of the 1961 publication of Jane Jacobs's "The Death and Life of Great American Cities". Jacobs's first book, it is her most widely read and most influential. Its thesis was that cities were problems of "organized complexity" which thrived on the unplanned variety of street life. It challenged existing city planning orthodoxy, which held that cities must be carefully planned to keep homes far from dirty, unsafe business and industry.

The city planners of the early 20th century criticized urban spaces as dirty and crowded - and rightly so. The slums of New York, Boston and London, centered around polluting factories, were unsanitary and unpleasant. The air and water were polluted, and tuberculosis, typhoid and cholera spread quickly. The streets were often filled with manure from horse-drawn carriages. People slept six or more to a room in crowded tenements. Reformers like Jacob Riis documented these conditions, and cities responded with tenement laws, sanitation systems, and eventually zoning codes to separate land uses.

From these conditions, a new movement towards city planning began to take shape. Ebenezer Howard's "Garden City", Daniel Burnham's "City Beautiful" and Le Corbusier's "Radiant City" all imagined that cities could be planned to reduce overcrowding and pollution. Le Corbusier, in particular, envisioned widely-spaced high-rise towers in parks where residents would live, separate from the business towers and factories where they worked, with people traveling between them by car or mass transit. 

Following World War II, housing shortages led to a building boom that took up many of these principles. In Manhattan, huge complexes like Stuyvesant Town and Peter Cooper Village, and public housing like the Lillian Wald Houses and Jacob Riis houses followed Le Corbusier's tower-in-a-park model, though unlike in Le Corbusier's vision, they stood within the city. Working-class neighborhoods were demolished to build these, and existing residents were displaced. The Housing Act of 1949 funded "slum clearance", later called urban renewal, which in New York was run by Robert Moses. Manhattan's San Juan Hill, on the Upper West Side, another working-class neighborhood made up primarily of African-American, Afro-Caribbean and Puerto Rican residents, was cleared to make way for Lincoln Center.

Jacobs, who was raised in Scranton, Pennsylvania, moved to Greenwich Village in 1935 at 19. Through the 1950s she was a writer for Architectural Forum, where she saw this work up close and began to question the mainstream consensus on urban planning. Her neighborhood, which had been labeled a slum and marked for clearance, was, to her, no slum at all. Instead, it was a working-class neighborhood where the streets were always full of people, where neighbors kept each other safe and where homes, shops and workplaces sat side by side, keeping the sidewalks busy from morning to night.

###### Research underpinnings

Before we examine whether Greenwich Village has these 4 qualities, let's look at what the research says about whether they are essential. Jacobs is often characterized as a 'citizen journalist' whose writing was based on what she saw, rather than deriving from experiments or data. Though I think it would be a mistake to dismiss her observations based on the fact that she wasn't a researcher, in the decades since the book has been published, there have been researchers who have decided to take a more data-driven look at what she found.

In 2019, researchers in Barcelona used smartphone tracking to analyze Jacobs' urban vitality principles by tracking the daily walking itineraries of 241 young adults. The researchers created a JANE index, based on Jacobs' four factors and representing a synthetic indicator of urban vitality (Delclòs-Alió, X., Gutiérrez, A., Miralles-Guasch, C. (2019). “The urban vitality

measurements of the built environment in a Mediterranean metropolis”. Cities, 86, 220-
228. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cities.2018.09.021)

Their key finding? That neighborhoods that score high on the JANE index, possessing density, mixed-use, old/new building mix, high transit access and a lack of border vacuums attract pedestrians.

##### A Dense Concentration of People, Including residents
## A Dense Concentration of People, Including Residents
#### The disctrict must have a sufficiently dense concentration of people, for whatever purpose they may be there. This includes people there because of residence.

According to Jacobs, "dense concentrations of people are one of the necessary conditions for flourishing city diversity". In suburbs, "The only goods and cultural activities available will be those that the majority requires"(Professor Henry Denton, as reported in The New York Times, p. 262). Cities, however, could have enough population density to support even niche interests. For Jacobs, this meant at least 100 dwellings per acre.

By that measure, Greenwich Village is doing fine. Most census tracts in the neighborhood sit at or near that number today. The tract containing Washington Square Park comes in lowest, at around 78, with the rest ranging from 93 to 111 dwellings per acre. In some tracts there are even more dwellings per acre than in 1970, though NYU housing is likely propping up that number in at least one tract.

So no problem, right? More dwellings per acre than ever. 

Except Jacobs chose dwellings per acre as a measure over people per acre for a specific reason: She needed a measure that wouldn't confuse an overcrowded slum with a thriving, busy neighborhood. Though it was the right choice for 1961, using that measure today hides the real story.

At a time when New York City as a whole was adding a million residents, Greenwich Village's population was shrinking by 6.6%.

Why would one of New York's most desireable neighborhoods be shrinking?

Not for lack of demand. People weren't moving out because they no longer wanted to live there. Instead, household size was shrinking, from 1.67 on average in 1970 to 1.5 today.  Households with at least 3 members went from 13.4% of the population in 1970 to just 11.4% in 2020.

 Though the shrinking households story doesn't map cleanly onto Jacobs's 100 dwellings per acre number, the end result is exactly what she predicted.  There are no longer enough residents to sustain the niche interests, or even as many of the ordinary ones - grocery stores, hardware stores, laundromats. It's not a clean cause and effect - given the rise in online shopping, we might see the variety in stores decrease even with the population staying constant.


##### Mixed Use
## Mixed Uses
Jacobs's last condition for a vital neighborhood is mixed primary use. She thought for a neighborhood to work well, it needed to have more than one primary function, and preferably more than two. A mix of uses reasons for people to use the streets at all times of the day.

The Greenwich Village of Jacobs's time certainly did. It was a residential neighborhood, but also a neighborhood people worked in, a neighborhood people shopped in, and a neighborhood people ate and drank in. It had restaurants and bars, clothing stores and shoe stores, grocery stores and drugstores, laundromats and beauty parlors. It had small stores that catered to niche interests, and art galleries that were destinations for people outside the neighborhood. These different uses gave the residents reasons to leave their homes and explore the neighborhood, and they gave people living outside the neighborhood reasons to visit. As a result, the streets were lively and filled with people most of the day.
