## Commercial Space Available
Caffe Reggio, at 119 MacDougal Street, served the first cappuccino in America 1927. Though the 1902 imported espresso machine that made that first cappuncino is now just a display piece, the cafe is still serving them 99 years later. It's still early in the morning and the street is quiet. Across the street, at 128 MacDougal, a metal gate covers the street-level windows. A sign advertises "Commercial Space Available". It has been vacant since at least April, according to Google's Streetview. 
<!-- svg: -->
128 MacDougal is just one of dozens of vacant storefronts in Greenwich Village, many of which have been vacant for more than a year and a half — six consecutive quarters. The problem is now big enough that in June 2026, the city comptroller's office issued a report, titled *Who's Minding the storefronts? An Analysis of Storefront Vacancies in New York City*. This follows a 2017 report by then-State Senator Brad Hoylman, titled *Bleaker on Bleecker*, focusing particularly on storefront vacancies in Greenwich Village and Chelsea. The citywide storefront vacancy rate, a modest 4 percent in 2017 and comparable to the nationwide 4.08 percent, has been over 11 percent since 2023.(Office of the New York City Comptroller 2026)
<!-- svg: -->
While media outlets, politicians and economists have debated the exact cause, the reality of the problem is undisputed. When asked on Jeremiah Moss's blog *Vanishing New York* about pasting oversized Monopoly cards onto empty Manhattan storefronts, the street artist known as Symbol explained:
> "Everywhere I walked in Manhattan there were empty storefronts, and it seemed like one big game between landlords and tenants... Is there a better analogy for that than the game of Monopoly?"—*[Vanishing New York](https://vanishingnewyork.blogspot.com/2018/06/high-rent-blight-monopoly.html)*

One such Monopoly card had this to say:
 >"There are only two ultimate public powers in shaping and running American cities: votes and control of the money. To sound nicer, we may call these "public opinion" and "disbursement of funds," but they are still votes and money."

<!-- svg: -->
The text is not the artist’s own. It’s a quotation from Jane Jacobs's *The Death and Life of Great American Cities*.

Jacobs, who wrote about her beloved neighborhood in the late 1950s and early 1960s, recognized the importance of storefront businesses to the vitality of a neighborhood. 

>"The trust of a city street is formed over time from many, many little public sidewalk contacts. It grows out of people stopping by at the bar for a beer, getting advice from the grocer and giving advice to the newsstand man, comparing opinions with other customers at the bakery and nodding hello to the two boys drinking pop on the stoop; eying the girls while waiting to be called for dinner, admonishing the children, hearing about a job from the hardware man and borrowing a dollar from the druggist, admiring the new babies and sympathizing over the way a coat faded."(73)


##### Curtain Thesis
Greenwich Village incubated the '60s folk music boom, the East Coast beat movement, and the Stonewall uprising. It was businesses like the Gaslight Cafe, Kettle of Fish, and the Oscar Wilde Memorial Bookshop that provided space and opportunity for these movements to take hold.

If storefronts are so important to a neighborhood, why do so many sit vacant in Greenwich Village today? 


##### Section Context
## The Death and Life of Great American Cities
2026 marks the 65th anniversary of the 1961 publication of Jane Jacobs's *The Death and Life of Great American Cities*. Jacobs,who was raised in Scranton, Pennsylvania, moved to Greenwich Village in 1935 at 19. Through the 1950s she was a writer for Architectural Forum, where she began to question the mainstream consensus on urban planning. When she saw her vibrant, working class neighborhood marked for slum clearance, she turned her eye to the workings of the city. What made a district thrive? She came up with four conditions. 

##### Four Conditions	

### 1: Short Blocks
Most blocks must be short That is, streets and opportunities to turn corners must be frequent.

### 2: A mix of old and new buildings
The district must mingle buildings that vary in age and condition, including a good proportion of old ones so that they may vary the economic yield they must produce.

### 3: High Density
There must be a sufficiently dense concentration of people, for whatever purposes they may be there. This includes dense concentration in the case of people who are there because of residence.(196)

### 4:  Mixed Uses
The district... must serve more than one primary function; preferably more than two
These must insure the presence of people who go outdoors on different schedules and are in the place for different purposes, but who are able to use many facilities in common.

##### Short Blocks
<!-- svg: map-a-zoom-out -->
We will start with the factor that has changed the least in 65 years: short blocks. 
<!-- svg: -->
Jacobs writes that short blocks give pedestrians frequent opportunities to turn corners, which means more possible routes any person can take to a given destination. A resident who goes to the same place regularly (whether it's their subway stop or favorite grocery store) will often take different routes to get there on different days, if the short blocks allow it, giving them more opportunities to see different parts of the neighborhood. 
<!-- svg: map-a-zoom-in -->
Long blocks do the opposite, funneling people onto a few streets and leaving the rest quiet.
<!-- svg: chart-b -->
Built before the standard 1811 Manhattan grid of widely spaced avenues (approx. 600-900 feet apart) and closely-spaced streets (approx 200-260 feet apart), blocks in Greenwich Village range between 150 - 500 feet and average 290 feet, among the shortest of all neighborhoods in Manhattan.
In addition to providing more opportunities for pedestrians to turn corners, the short, irregular blocks of Greenwich Village provide 
>"visual interruptions, cutting off the indefinite distant view and at the same time visually heightening and celebrating intense street use by giving it a hint of enclosure and entity".
<!-- svg: -->
Short blocks can also help a pedestrian's feeling of safety. A person walking on a short block who gets a feeling of being unsafe, either because of a person or their physical surroundings, can find somewhere to turn within a couple hundred feet, whereas a person walking on a long avenue might have no choice but to turn in the opposite direction.

##### A Mixture of Old and New Buildings 
<!-- svg: map-c -->
Following Dutch and English colonization of Lenape land, Greenwich Village grew from farmland into a built-up neighborhood in the early 1800s, which left it some of Manhattan's oldest surviving buildings. Much of that early construction is still standing. Although parts of the neighborhood were threatened with 'urban renewal' in the 1950s and 1960s, which would have demolished many older buildings, residents defeated those plans. In 1969 the city designated the Greenwich Village Historic District, protecting many of the neighborhood's older buildings from demolition.
<!-- svg: chart-d -->
Roughly 40 percent of the buildings in Greenwich Village were built before 1861. Another 50 percent were built between 1861 and 1929. Only about 10 percent of Greenwich Village's buildings were built after 1929. This lends the neighborhood a wide range of architectural styles.
When Jacobs writes about a mixture of old and new buildings, though, it isn’t only for the purpose of visual diversity. Primarily, she writes that the buildings should 
>“vary in age and condition, including a good proportion of old ones so that they vary in the economic yield they must produce.” (197)

Just as there was a large variation in age of buildings, there was also a large variation in asking rents, with per square foot prices ranging from $2.33 per square foot per year to $9.68, according to New York Times Classified ads. Add in the fact that would-be renters could choose a larger or smaller storefront, depending on their needs, and you find that a prospective renter could pay as little as $68 per month in 1961 for a 350 square foot space, or as much as $350 per month for a larger, possibly nicer space. 

<!-- svg: chart-e e1 -->
Jacobs expected old buildings to supply the lower end of the rent range. In the first half of 2024, according to REBNY, The Real Estate Board of New York, asking rents for storefronts on Bleecker Street between 7th Avenue South and Houston ranged from $160 to $195 per square foot, with the most expensive space costing only 22% more than the least expensive one. This is despite almost all the buildings on that stretch having been built before 1930.

<!-- svg: chart-e e1a -->
Although Greenwich Village is still a place with both old and new buildings, the buildings now do not vary in the economic yield they must produce. There is little difference between the cheapest and most expensive storefronts, leaving small and less profitable businesses priced out entirely.

<!-- svg: chart-e e2 -->
Though rents are not public record, several have been documented on Vanishing New York and in articles in the New York Times, establishing a link between businesses that have closed and the surge in rents.

<!-- svg: chart-k -->

Nusraty Afghan Imports moved from Bleecker Street to Christopher Street in 2008 after the rent was raised from $7,000 to $45,000/month.(NYTimes). The store closed for good in 2020.

##### A Dense Concentration of People, Including Residents
<!-- svg: chart-h1-->
According to Jacobs, 
>"dense concentrations of people are one of the necessary conditions for flourishing city diversity". 

In suburbs, 

>"The only goods and cultural activities available will be those that the majority requires"(Professor Henry Denton, as reported in The New York Times, p. 262).

Cities, however, could have enough population density to support even niche interests. For Jacobs, this meant at least 100 dwellings per acre. Jacobs chose dwellings per acre as her metric rather than population because she needed a metric that wouldn't confuse an overcrowded slum with a dense and vibrant neighborhood, both of which could have high populations. 

<!-- svg: chart-h1-->
By that measure, Greenwich Village is doing fine. Most census tracts in the neighborhood sit at or near that number today. The tract containing Washington Square Park comes in lowest, at around 78, with the rest ranging from 93 to 111 dwellings per acre. In some tracts there are even more dwellings per acre than in 1970, though NYU housing is likely propping up that number in at least one tract.

<!-- svg: -->
At the same time, however, Greenwich Village's population dropped by 6.6%, at a time when Manhattan as a whole was adding more than half a million residents.
<!-- svg: -->
Why would one of New York's most desireable neighborhoods be shrinking?

Not for lack of demand. People weren't moving out because they no longer wanted to live there. Instead, household size was shrinking, from 1.67 on average in 1970 to 1.5 today.  Households with at least 3 members went from 13.4% of the population in 1970 to just 11.4% in 2020.
<!-- svg: chart-h1-->
Though the shrinking households story doesn't map cleanly onto Jacobs's 100 dwellings per acre number, it points to a similar concern: Fewer residents means fewer people to support the variety of businesses that once lined the street. That problem is compounded by the rising rents we've already seen. A business can't sustain itself when it has both rising expenses and a decreasing number of customers. Add in online shopping, and it becomes very difficult for niche businesses to stay open.

##### Mixed Uses
<!-- svg: chart-j -->
Jacobs compared Greenwich Village, whose streets were used by residents, workers and visitors alike, giving it a wide variety of uses, to a single-use district, the Financial District, which mostly was used for work. 
>"... the district is miserable at providing services and amenities proportional to the need. Its eating places and clothing shops are pitifully inadequate in number and variety for the demands on them. The district used to have one of the best hardware stores in New York, but a few years ago it could no longer make ends meet, and closed."(201) Without all-day use from residents and visitors, interesting shops and restaurants could not sustain themselves.
Jacobs's last condition for a vital neighborhood is mixed primary uses. She thought for a neighborhood to work well, it needed to have more than one primary function, and preferably more than two. A mix of uses meant that people would be using the streets at all times of the day, bringing money into the district. 
Of Greenwich Village, Jacobs wrote, 
>"The workers from the laboratories, meat-packing plants, warehouses, plus those from a bewildering variety of small manufacturers, printers and other little industries and offices, give all the eating places and much of the other commerce support at midday. We residents on the street and on its more purely residential tributaries could and would support a modicum of commerce by ourselves, but relatively little. We possess more convenience, liveliness, variety and choice than we 'deserve' in our own right... We support these things together by unconsciously cooperating economically."(199). 
This is backed up by the listings from Cole's Cross-Reference Directory. The 1971 volume, the first I could access, lists dozens of types of shops, from XX to XX. We also know from the density data that Greenwich Village was densely populated, and its volume of restaurants and other amenities suggest that the neighborhood was popular with visitors.
<!-- svg: map-g -->
The Greenwich Village of the 1960s and 1970s was a residential neighborhood, but also a neighborhood people worked in, a neighborhood people shopped in, and a neighborhood people ate and drank in. It had restaurants and bars, clothing stores and shoe stores, grocery stores and drugstores, laundromats and beauty parlors. It had small stores that catered to niche interests, and art galleries that were destinations for people outside the neighborhood. These different uses gave the residents reasons to leave their homes and explore the neighborhood, and they gave people living outside the neighborhood reasons to visit. As a result, the streets were lively and filled with people most of the day.

In 2026, many storefronts sit vacant. Others are occupied by the types of chains that residents of other neighborhoods would have no reason to travel to go to visit. Only a couple art galleries remain. The contrast between the two eras show why Jacobs considered mixed primary uses essential. In the 1960s and 1970s, Greenwich Village's variety of uses drew residents out of their homes, kept workers in the neighborhood, and drew visitors from other neighborhoods. Today, vacant storefronts, chains, and a high rate of restaurant turnover suggest that the Village may no longer support the "unconscious cooperation" Jacobs described. Stores, like parks, need users, and the Village's changing streetscape suggests it may have lost some of the users it once had.

##### Why It's Happening
## Why It's Happening

To understand why so many storefronts sit empty in high-rent districts like Greenwich Village, it helps to know a little about the concept that economists call option value. In economics, option value refers to the economic value a property owner generates by retaining the flexibility to make a decision later rather than committing immediately to a long-term asset lock.

Imagine a landlord who suddenly has a vacant storefront for rent. It is the year 2008. They have potential tenants, who are willing to pay $130/square foot per year, comparable with rents in the past. However, this landlord knows that Marc Jacobs recently rented several storefronts just down the street for $600/square foot. By locking in a 10-year lease at $130/square foot, the landlord loses out on the possibility of getting $600/square foot for his storefront, a loss of many thousands of dollars over the next 10 years. You can easily imagine the landlord deciding not to rent the storefront out at $130, or $140, or $150 per square foot, instead choosing to wait for a tenant willing to pay more. 

When you imagine 10, 20 or 100 landlords all making the same calculation, suddenly it's easy to see how storefronts could sit empty for years on end.

In examining storefront vacancy in New York City, economists Erica Moszkowski and Daniel Stackman cite two sources of uncertainty which generate option value for landlords. The first, time series uncertainty, states that in a market where rents are often cyclical, landlords have an incentive to sign tenants as close to the top of the rent cycle as possible. The second, cross-sectional heterogeneity in tenant willingness to pay, also referred to as "match quality", states that there is substantial variation between what any two given tenants are willing to pay for a given space, creating an incentive for the landlord to wait for a higher-paying tenant rather than lease to a lower-paying one. (Moszkowski & Stackman, 2026)

After examining all these factors, we can put together a timeline and a hypothesis.

Between 1961 and 2001, commercial rents on Bleecker Street and across Greenwich Village moved in tandem with commercial rents in Manhattan as a whole.

In July of 2000, Carrie Bradshaw and Miranda Hobbes eat cupcakes outside the original Magnolia Bakery at 401 Bleecker Street on "Sex and the City", turning it into an overnight sensation that drew massive crowds to Bleecker Street.

In 2001, Marc Jacobs begins opening boutiques on Bleecker Street, soon followed by Ralph Lauren and Cynthia Rowley. In response to Bleecker Street's new popularity and rising rents, REBNY (The Real Estate Board of New York) added Bleecker Street to the commercial corridors they tracked, putting it in the company of Madison Avenue and 5th Avenue, among others. At this point mean asking rent on Bleecker Street was between $265 and $600 per square foot per year, with a mean of $397.

As older leases expired, landlords raised rents to keep up with the going market rate in the neighborhood, leaving stores vacant if there was no high-rent tenant willing to immediately take over the lease.  

The profits the high-fashion brands expected to see never materialized, though, and they began to pull out of the neighborhood.

By 2010, 5 of 6 Bleecker Street Marc Jacobs stores were closed. 

Landlords, still holding out for that $600 rent, were slow to realize that that number might be unrealistic. Asking rents tumbled between 2013 and 2024 from a mean of $xxxx in 2009 to a mean of $xxxx in 2021, their storefronts sitting vacant in many cases for months or years on end. 


##### Putting Everything Together
## Putting Everything Together
So now that we've looked at all 4 conditions, what can we say about the changes in Greenwich Village over the last 65 years?

### Fewer types of businesses
Independent bookstores, record stores, musical instrument stores, and specialty food stores have all declined, replaced by the types of businesses that do little to drive cross-neighborhood foot traffic. 

### Dramatically higher rents
Sparked by the 2000s retail boom, landlords raised storefront rents past what independent businesses could bear. Because commercial leases are most often 10 years long, many businesses who had last renewed their leases before 20000 faced huge increases when their next renewal rolled around. Though I don't have systematic data on this, Jeremiah Moss's blog "Vanishing New York" chronicles many of the rent raises that lead to closures, and the New York Times interviewed several other business owners, giving us a few more data points. 



### More vacancies
While I don't have concrete vacancy data from before 2007, historical context shows that commercial vacancy was not a notable issue before then.  Firstly, we have no documentation that vacancy in Greenwich Village was considered a problem before the mid-2000s. Second, the ground-floor commercial vacancy rate in the West Village was around 1.9% in 2007(https://comptroller.nyc.gov/reports/retail-vacancy-in-new-york-city/), less than the citywide average of 4%, and there are no indications that this was a deviation from long-term trends. Third, the data we do have shows storefront vacancy rising from roughly 1.9 percent in 2007 to roughly 13 percent today. 

The rise in vacancies follows the rise in rents. The businesses listed above closed after their rents went up, and the spaces they left have been harder to fill than they used to be, often staying vacant more than 18 months. Taken together, the three changes point the same way. There are fewer types of business, the rents are much higher, and more storefronts are empty. Though the physical conditions of Greenwich Village have not changed, the economic conditions that made it possible for small, independent, and unique businesses to thrive have disappeared.

###### Research underpinnings
## Do the four conditions matter?
Jacobs was not a researcher in the conventional sense. Though she completed classwork at Columbia, she left without earning a degree. Her expertise came from observing the city around her and drawing conclusions from what she saw. It's fair to ask whether there is any empirical evidence that her four conditions actually shape the economic success and social cohesion of a district, or if they are merely a romanticized snapshot of 1950s Greenwich Village.

In recent years, researchers have begun putting her theories to the test. In 2019, researchers in Barcelona used smartphone tracking to analyze urban vitality by tracking the daily walking itineraries of 241 young adults. They created a "JANE index", a synthetic indicator based on Jacobs' conditions, and found that neighborhoods scoring high on the index(possessing density, mixed primary uses, a mix of old and new buildings, high transit access, and a lack of border vacuums) strongly correlate with increased pedestrian activity (Delclòs-Alió et al., 2019). 

So neighborhoods with Jacobs's conditions have more pedestrian density. But does that increased foot traffic lead to the type of trust in your neighbors and community that Jacobs described in "The Death and Life of Great American Cities"? And does that type of trust make people happier?

In "Happy City", Charles Montgomery examined those two questions. Looking at research from around the world, he found that "People who live in monofunctional, car-dependent neighborhoods outside of urban centers are much less trusting of other people than people who live in walkable neighborhoods where housing is mixed with shops, services, and places to work"(need proper cite).

Based on this research, there is evidence to say that residents' happiness in their neighborhood and trust in their neighbors is correlated with Jacobs's four conditions.

Jacobs argued that this trust accumulates through small, everyday contacts in the shops, bars, and restaurants of a mixed-use neighborhood, as well as on the sidewalks between those places. When those businesses leave, the networks of trust and community that they have nurtured erode.

##### Conclusion
## Conclusion

When I embarked on this project I was ready to say that the gentrification and high-rent blight of Greenwich Village was something that Jacobs could never have predicted.Yet, in "The Death and Life of Great American Cities", she maps out the exact feedback loop currently playing out in the neighborhood. In a chapter called "The Self-Destruction of Diversity", Jacobs writes,
"Whichever form the self-destruction takes, this, in broad strokes, is what happens: A diversified mixture of uses at some place in the city becomes outstandingly popular and successful as a whole. Because of the location's success, which is invariably based on flourishing and magnetic diversity, ardent competition for space in this locality develops. It is taken up in what amounts to the economic equivalent of a fad. The winners in the competition for space will represent only a narrow segment of the many uses that together created success...Thus, from this process, one or few dominating uses finally emerge triumphant. But the triumph is hollow. A most intricate and successful organism of economic mutual support and social mutual support has been destroyed by this process... In time, a place that was once so successful and once the object of such ardent competition, wanes and becomes marginal."

Decades later, the experiences of local business owners echo her warning. When Richard Geist closed Uncle Sam's Army/Navy Store on West 8th street after decades in business, he blamed reduced foot traffic rather than high rents. As chronicled by Jeremiah Moss on Vanishing New York: 
>"Geist told me the closure is by choice, mostly, and that 8th Street just isn't 8th Street anymore... High rents wiped out the shoe businesses here, leaving a blighted ghost town about 10 years ago... Geist says there's no foot traffic. The street is dead." (https://vanishingnewyork.blogspot.com/2018/12/uncle-sams-armynavy.html). 

The year before, Geist had told the New York Times, 
>"Gentrification is killing us. Eighth Street has lost the magic" (as cited in Moss, [2018]).

The lost magic is not unique to 8th Street or to Greenwich Village. Across walkable, mixed-use neighborhoods in New York and beyond, independent storefronts are being displaced by capital-backed chains: fast-casual salad and burrito places, corporate coffee shops, and cookie-cutter retail. When financial speculation and monoculture strip away the fine-grained variety of the street, they destroy the very diversity that made the neighborhood resilient in the first place. But we can all play a part in bringing that magic back - by choosing to make a difference in our choices of where we shop, how we interact with our community, and how we come together when our neighbors need us.

As Jacobs observed,
>"Cities have the capability of providing something for everybody, only because, and only when, they are created by everybody."
